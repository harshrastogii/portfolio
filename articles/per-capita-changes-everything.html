Per-capita changes everything

Why switching from raw counts to a rate quietly reorders who your data says is most affected — and who then gets served by it.

Every dashboard makes an argument before anyone reads a single number. The argument is in the choice of denominator — and most of the time nobody chose it on purpose.

The default is a decision

When you plot recorded offences by community as raw counts, the largest towns rise to the top. That feels intuitive: more people, more incidents. But "where do the most things happen" and "where is daily life most affected" are different questions, and a count answers only the first.

Divide by population and the order can invert entirely. A small community with a few hundred residents and a modest absolute count can sit far above a regional centre once you express the figure as a rate. Neither view is wrong. They answer different questions — and the one you ship is the one your audience will treat as the truth.

Why this matters beyond the chart

Resourcing decisions follow the picture. If the top of your chart is always the biggest town, attention flows there by default, and smaller communities carrying a higher per-person burden stay invisible precisely because they're small. The framing doesn't just describe who is affected; it shapes who gets served.

Three guards I now apply

After watching a per-capita toggle reorder a Territory crime dashboard, I started treating the denominator as a first-class design decision rather than an afterthought:

  • Show both, label both. Offer count and rate as an explicit switch, so the reader knows a choice is being made and can make it themselves.
  • Name the denominator. "Per 1,000 residents" in the axis title, every time. An unlabelled rate is just a different way to mislead.
  • Watch small populations. Rates over tiny denominators are volatile; a single incident can spike a per-capita figure. Flag low-population areas rather than letting them top the chart on noise.
How you frame data shapes who gets served by it.

The takeaway

None of this is advanced statistics. It's the opposite — it's the part so basic that it gets skipped. But the denominator is where a chart quietly decides whose reality it represents, and that decision deserves to be made in the open. Pick it deliberately, label it plainly, and let the reader see the choice you made.
